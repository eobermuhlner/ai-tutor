## Lesson Goals

- Master common IPA diacritics for articulation modification

## Grammar Focus

### Stress Marking

**Rule:** Stress (prominence) is marked with vertical marks before the stressed syllable.

**Stress symbols:**
- **[ˈ]** - Primary stress (vertical stroke above): placed **before** the stressed syllable
- **[ˌ]** - Secondary stress (vertical stroke below): placed **before** the syllable

Examples:
- "about" **[əˈbaʊt]** - stress on second syllable
- "photograph" **[ˈfoʊtəɡɹæf]** - stress on first syllable
- "photographer" **[fəˈtɑɡɹəfɚ]** - stress on second syllable
- "photographic" **[ˌfoʊtəˈɡɹæfɪk]** - secondary stress on first, primary on third

**Stress shift examples:**

English words show stress shift in related forms:
- "PREsent" (noun) **[ˈpɹɛzn̩t]** vs. "preSENT" (verb) **[pɹɪˈzɛnt]**
- "REcord" (noun) **[ˈɹɛkɚd]** vs. "reCORD" (verb) **[ɹɪˈkɔɹd]**
- "PHOtograph" **[ˈfoʊtəɡɹæf]** → "phoTOGraphy" **[fəˈtɑɡɹəfi]**

Spanish stress:
- "háblame" (speak to me) **[ˈaβlame]** - stress on first syllable
- "hablé" (I spoke) **[aˈβle]** - stress on final syllable

### Syllable Boundaries

**[.]** - Syllable boundary: marks division between syllables

Examples:
- Spanish "casa" **[ˈka.sa]** - two syllables
- English "destroy" **[dɪ.ˈstɹɔɪ]** - two syllables with stress on second
- German "Haus.tür" (front door) **[ˈhaʊs.tyːɐ]** - compound with syllable boundary

### Length Marks

**Rule:** Length is marked with colon-like symbols after the sound being lengthened.

**Length symbols:**
- **[ː]** - Long (triangular colon): Spanish "sí" **[siː]**, German "Beet" **[beːt]**
- **[ˑ]** - Half-long: less common, marks intermediate length
- **[◌̆]** - Extra-short (breve diacritic over sound)

Examples showing length contrasts:
- German "Beet" (flowerbed) **[beːt]** vs. "Bett" (bed) **[bɛt]**
- German "Ofen" (oven) **[ˈoːfn̩]** vs. "offen" (open) **[ˈɔfn̩]**
- Japanese "おばさん" (aunt) **[obaːsaɴ]** vs. "おばあさん" (grandmother) **[obaːːsaɴ]**
- Finnish "kuka" (who) **[ˈkukɑ]** vs. "kukka" (flower) **[ˈkukːɑ]** - long consonant

### Tone Marks

**Rule:** Tone marks indicate pitch height and contour on vowels or syllables. Common in Asian and African languages.

**Tone letter system (Chao system):**

Uses vertical scale 1-5 (1=lowest, 5=highest):
- **[◌˥]** or **[55]** - High level tone
- **[◌˧]** or **[33]** - Mid level tone
- **[◌˩]** or **[11]** - Low level tone
- **[◌˥˩]** or **[51]** - High falling tone
- **[◌˩˥]** or **[15]** - Low rising tone

**Alternative tone marking (diacritics):**

- **[◌̄]** - High level (macron)
- **[◌́]** - Rising tone (acute accent)
- **[◌̀]** - Falling tone (grave accent)
- **[◌̂]** - Fall-rise/rise-fall (circumflex)
- **[◌̌]** - Rising contour (caron/hacek)

**Mandarin Chinese examples (using tone numbers):**

- 妈 "mother" **[ma˥]** or **[ma1]** - high level (tone 1)
- 麻 "hemp" **[ma˧˥]** or **[ma2]** - rising (tone 2)
- 马 "horse" **[ma˨˩˦]** or **[ma3]** - dipping (tone 3)
- 骂 "scold" **[ma˥˩]** or **[ma4]** - falling (tone 4)
- 吗 "question particle" **[ma]** - neutral tone

**Thai examples:**

- "mid tone" **[maː˧]**
- "low tone" **[maː˩]**
- "falling tone" **[maː˥˩]**
- "high tone" **[maː˥]**
- "rising tone" **[maː˩˥]**

### Voice Quality Diacritics

**Rule:** Diacritics mark variations in phonation (voice quality).

**Common voice quality marks:**

- **[◌̥]** - Voiceless (ring below): **[l̥]**, **[w̥]** - voiceless sonorants
- **[◌̬]** - Voiced (wedge below): **[s̬]** - voiced **[s]** (like **[z]**)
- **[◌̤]** - Breathy voice (two dots below): **[b̤a]** - breathy voiced
- **[◌̰]** - Creaky voice (tilde below): **[b̰a]** - creaky/laryngealized
- **[ʔ]** + vowel - Glottalized vowel: **[ʔa]**

Examples:
- English "please" (devoiced **[l]**) **[pʰl̥iz]** in rapid speech
- Hindi "घ" (breathy voiced **[ɡ]**) **[ɡ̤]**
- Burmese creaky tone: **[a̰]**

### Articulation Modification Diacritics

**Rule:** Diacritics modify the place, manner, or secondary articulation of consonants.

**Place/manner modifications:**

| Diacritic | Name | Example |
|-----------|------|---------|
| **[◌̪]** | Dental (bridge below) | **[t̪]** - dental **[t]** (tongue at teeth) |
| **[◌̺]** | Apical (point below) | **[t̺]** - apical **[t]** (tongue tip) |
| **[◌̻]** | Laminal (square below) | **[t̻]** - laminal **[t]** (tongue blade) |
| **[◌̼]** | Linguolabial | **[t̼]** - tongue touches upper lip |
| **[◌̹]** | More rounded | **[ɔ̹]** - extra rounding |
| **[◌̜]** | Less rounded | **[ɔ̜]** - reduced rounding |
| **[◌̟]** | Advanced (plus below) | **[u̟]** - fronted **[u]** |
| **[◌̠]** | Retracted (minus below) | **[i̠]** - backed **[i]** |
| **[◌̈]** | Centralized (diaeresis) | **[ɪ̈]** - centralized **[ɪ]** |

Examples:
- Spanish "t" is dental **[t̪]**, English "t" is alveolar **[t]**
- French "tu" **[t̪y]** - dental stop with rounded front vowel

**Secondary articulation:**

| Diacritic | Name | Example | Description |
|-----------|------|---------|-------------|
| **[◌ʷ]** | Labialized | **[kʷ]** | With lip rounding |
| **[◌ʲ]** | Palatalized | **[tʲ]** | With tongue raising toward palate |
| **[◌ˠ]** | Velarized | **[ɫ]**, **[tˠ]** | With tongue raising toward velum |
| **[◌ˤ]** | Pharyngealized | **[tˤ]** | With tongue root retraction |
| **[◌̃]** | Nasalized | **[ã]** | With nasal airflow |

Examples:
- Russian "тя" **[tʲa]** - palatalized **[t]**
- Arabic "ط" **[tˤ]** - pharyngealized (emphatic) **[t]**
- English "bottle" **[ˈbɑtɫ̩]** - velarized (dark) **[l]**
- French "bon" **[bɔ̃]** - nasalized vowel

### Release Diacritics

**[◌̚]** - No audible release: closure formed but not released

Examples:
- English "apt" **[æp̚t]** - unreleased **[p]**
- Korean "밥" (rice) **[pap̚]** - unreleased final **[p]**

**[◌ʰ]** - Aspirated: strong breath release

Examples:
- English "top" **[tʰɑp]** - aspirated **[t]**
- Mandarin "他" (he) **[tʰa˥]** - aspirated **[t]**

### Intonation and Breaks

**Intonation symbols:**

- **[↗]** - Rising intonation (question): "Really?" **[ˈɹɪli ↗]**
- **[↘]** - Falling intonation (statement): "Yes." **[jɛs ↘]**
- **[→]** - Level intonation: continuation

**Prosodic breaks:**

- **[|]** - Minor (foot) break
- **[‖]** - Major (intonation) break: between phrases or sentences

Examples:
- "I went to the store | and bought some milk ‖"
- **[aɪ wɛnt tə ðə stɔɹ | ænd bɔt səm mɪɫk ‖]**

## Vocabulary

### Suprasegmental Terms
- **Suprasegmental** - Features extending over more than one segment (stress, tone, intonation)
- **Stress** - Prominence or emphasis on a syllable
- **Tone** - Pitch pattern distinguishing word meaning
- **Intonation** - Pitch contour over phrase or sentence
- **Length** - Duration of a sound (short vs. long)

### Diacritic Types
- **Diacritic** - Small mark modifying a base symbol
- **Voicing diacritic** - Marks voiceless or voiced quality
- **Nasalization** - Airflow through nasal cavity
- **Palatalization** - Secondary articulation with tongue raising
- **Velarization** - Secondary articulation with tongue backing
- **Labialization** - Secondary articulation with lip rounding

### Phonetic Symbols (Suprasegmentals)
- **[ˈ]** - Primary stress
- **[ˌ]** - Secondary stress
- **[ː]** - Long
- **[.]** - Syllable boundary
- **[↗]**, **[↘]** - Intonation contours
- **[|]**, **[‖]** - Prosodic breaks

### Common Diacritics
- **[◌̥]** - Voiceless
- **[◌̃]** - Nasalized
- **[◌ʷ]** - Labialized
- **[◌ʲ]** - Palatalized
- **[◌̚]** - Unreleased
- **[◌ʰ]** - Aspirated
- **[◌̪]** - Dental
- **[◌̤]** - Breathy voiced
- **[◌̰]** - Creaky voiced

## Conversation Scenarios

### Analyzing Stress Patterns

Practice identifying primary and secondary stress in multi-syllable words.

Key phrases:
- "The primary stress is on the second syllable"
- "There's secondary stress on the first syllable"
- "Stress shifts from the first to the second syllable in this word pair"

### Recognizing Tone and Length

Practice identifying tone marks and length distinctions in tonal languages.

Key phrases:
- "This vowel has high level tone marked with **[˥]**"
- "The long vowel is marked with **[ː]**"
- "Mandarin tone 4 is a high falling tone"

## Practice Patterns

1. **Stress marking:** Mark stress in multi-syllable words: "photography" → **[fəˈtɑɡɹəfi]**
2. **Length contrasts:** Distinguish German short vs. long vowels: **[bet]** vs. **[beːt]**
3. **Tone identification:** Recognize Mandarin tones: **[ma˥]**, **[ma˧˥]**, **[ma˨˩˦]**, **[ma˥˩]**
4. **Diacritic application:** Add diacritics: Spanish dental **[t̪]**, nasalized **[ã]**, aspir ated **[pʰ]**
5. **Secondary articulation:** Transcribe palatalized, velarized, and labialized consonants
6. **Syllable division:** Mark syllable boundaries: "destroy" → **[dɪ.ˈstɹɔɪ]**
7. **Complex transcription:** Read transcriptions with multiple diacritics: **[pʰl̥iːz]** (please)

## Common Mistakes to Watch

- Placing stress mark after syllable → Stress **[ˈ]** goes **before** the syllable: **[əˈbaʊt]** not **[əbaʊˈt]**
- Confusing **[ː]** and **[:]** → Use triangular colon **[ː]** not regular colon **[:]**
- Missing length in German → "Beet" needs **[beːt]** with **[ː]**, not **[bet]**
- **[◌̃] on consonants → Nasalization diacritic goes on **vowels**: **[ã]**, **[õ]**, not consonants
- Wrong tone marks → Mandarin uses 5-level system: **[ma˥]** (high) vs. **[ma˩]** (low)
- Confusing **[◌̥]** and **[◌̊]** → Use subscript ring **[◌̥]** for voiceless, not superscript
- **[ʰ]** on voiced sounds → Aspiration **[ʰ]** applies to voiceless plosives: **[pʰ]**, **[tʰ]**, **[kʰ]**
- Forgetting syllable breaks → "destroy" is **[dɪ.ˈstɹɔɪ]** (two syllables), not **[dɪstɹɔɪ]**
- Misplacing **[◌̪]** → Dental diacritic goes **under** the symbol: **[t̪]** not **[t̪]** above
- Confusing **[◌ʷ]** (labialized) and **[w]** (consonant) → **[kʷ]** is modified **[k]**, not **[k]** + **[w]**

## Cultural Notes

- English word stress is unpredictable and must be memorized, unlike Spanish which follows regular rules
- Tone languages (Mandarin, Thai, Vietnamese, Yoruba) use pitch to distinguish word meanings - crucial for communication
- Japanese pitch accent is different from Chinese tone: only high vs. low, not contour tones
- Aspiration of voiceless plosives is strong in English/German, absent in Spanish/French - major accent marker
- Russian and Irish have pervasive palatalization contrasts ("hard" vs. "soft" consonants)
- The IPA has over 100 diacritics for fine phonetic detail beyond basic symbols
- Some languages (Finnish, Japanese, Italian) have geminate (long) consonants as phonemes: "kk", "tt", "pp"
- French nasal vowels lost their nasal consonants historically: Latin "bonum" → French "bon" **[bɔ̃]**
