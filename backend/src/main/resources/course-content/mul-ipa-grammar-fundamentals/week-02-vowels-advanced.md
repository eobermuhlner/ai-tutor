## This Week's Goals

- Master near-close and near-open vowel symbols beyond the cardinal vowels

## Grammar Focus

### Near-Close and Near-Open Vowels

**Rule:** Between the cardinal vowel positions lie additional vowel symbols representing sounds common in many languages.

**Near-close vowels** (between close and close-mid):

| Symbol | Description | Language examples |
|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **[ɪ]** | Near-close front unrounded | English "sit" **[sɪt]**, "city" **[ˈsɪti]** |
| **[ʏ]** | Near-close front rounded | German "hübsch" (pretty) **[hʏpʃ]** |
| **[ʊ]** | Near-close back rounded | English "book" **[bʊk]**, "good" **[ɡʊd]** |

**Near-open vowels** (between open-mid and open):

| Symbol | Description | Language examples |
|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **[æ]** | Near-open front unrounded | English "cat" **[kʰæt]**, "bad" **[bæd]** |
| **[ɐ]** | Near-open central | German "besser" (better) **[ˈbɛsɐ]**, Australian English "but" |

Contrasts:
- English "beat" **[biːt]** (close **[i]**) vs. "bit" **[bɪt]** (near-close **[ɪ]**)
- English "pool" **[pʰuːl]** (close **[u]**) vs. "pull" **[pʰʊl]** (near-close **[ʊ]**)
- English "bed" **[bɛd]** (open-mid **[ɛ]**) vs. "bad" **[bæd]** (near-open **[æ]**)

Examples in sentences:
- English: "The big fish sit in the deep sea" **[ðə bɪɡ fɪʃ sɪt ɪn ðə diːp siː]**
- German: "Das Mütterchen" (little mother) **[das ˈmʏtɐçən]**

### Rounded Front Vowels

**Rule:** Some languages have front vowels produced with lip rounding, creating sounds not found in English.

**Complete rounded front vowel series:**

| Symbol | Description | Language examples |
|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **[y]** | Close front rounded | French "tu" (you) **[ty]**, German "über" (over) **[ˈyːbɐ]** |
| **[ø]** | Close-mid front rounded | French "peu" (few) **[pø]**, German "schön" (beautiful) **[ʃøːn]** |
| **[œ]** | Open-mid front rounded | French "peur" (fear) **[pœʁ]**, German "können" (can) **[ˈkœnən]** |
| **[ʏ]** | Near-close front rounded | German "Glück" (luck) **[ɡlʏk]** |

**Production tip:** To produce **[y]**, say **[i]** (as in "see") while rounding your lips as for **[u]** (as in "boot").

French examples:
- "tu" (you) **[ty]** - close front rounded
- "tout" (all) **[tu]** - close back rounded (contrast: front vs. back)
- "thé" (tea) **[te]** - close-mid front unrounded (contrast: rounded vs. unrounded)

German examples:
- "fühlen" (to feel) **[ˈfyːlən]** - long close front rounded
- "füllen" (to fill) **[ˈfʏlən]** - short near-close front rounded
- "Höhle" (cave) **[ˈhøːlə]** - long close-mid front rounded
- "Hölle" (hell) **[ˈhœlə]** - short open-mid front rounded

### Diphthongs

**Rule:** A diphthong is a vowel sound that glides from one vowel quality to another within a single syllable. Transcribed as two vowel symbols.

**Common English diphthongs:**

| Symbol | As in word | Phonetic transcription | Glide direction |
|--------|------------|------------------------|-----------------|
| **[aɪ]** | "price", "my" | **[pɹaɪs]**, **[maɪ]** | open → close front |
| **[aʊ]** | "mouth", "now" | **[maʊθ]**, **[naʊ]** | open → close back |
| **[eɪ]** | "face", "day" | **[feɪs]**, **[deɪ]** | close-mid → close front |
| **[əʊ]** | "goat", "no" (UK) | **[ɡəʊt]**, **[nəʊ]** | central → close back |
| **[oʊ]** | "goat", "no" (US) | **[ɡoʊt]**, **[noʊ]** | close-mid → close back |
| **[ɔɪ]** | "choice", "boy" | **[tʃɔɪs]**, **[bɔɪ]** | open-mid back → close front |

**British vs. American English diphthong differences:**

- "goat": British **[ɡəʊt]** vs. American **[ɡoʊt]**
- "face": British **[feɪs]** vs. American **[feɪs]** (similar)
- "price": British **[pɹaɪs]** vs. American **[pɹaɪs]** (similar)

**Additional diphthongs in other languages:**

| Symbol | Language | Example | Meaning |
|--------|----------|---------|---------|
| **[ai]** | Spanish | "baile" **[ˈbaile]** | dance |
| **[au]** | Spanish | "auto" **[ˈauto]** | car |
| **[ɔʏ]** | German | "heute" **[ˈhɔʏtə]** | today |
| **[aʊ]** | German | "Haus" **[haʊs]** | house |
| **[ɛi]** | German | "nein" **[naɪn]** or **[nɛɪn]** | no |

Examples in phrases:
- English: "The boy enjoys the loud noise" **[ðə bɔɪ ɪnˈdʒɔɪz ðə laʊd nɔɪz]**
- German: "Heute kaufe ich ein neues Haus" **[ˈhɔʏtə ˈkaʊfə ɪç aɪn ˈnɔʏəs haʊs]**

### Triphthongs

**Rule:** Triphthongs are sequences of three vowel qualities gliding within one syllable. Rare but found in some English words.

**English triphthongs:**

| Symbol | Example | Transcription |
|--------|---------|---------------|
| **[aɪə]** | "fire" (careful pronunciation) | **[ˈfaɪə]** |
| **[aʊə]** | "hour" (careful pronunciation) | **[ˈaʊə]** |
| **[eɪə]** | "player" | **[ˈpleɪə]** |
| **[ɔɪə]** | "loyal" | **[ˈlɔɪəl]** |

Note: In rapid speech, triphthongs often simplify:
- "fire": careful **[ˈfaɪə]** → rapid **[faɪɹ]** (with consonantal **[ɹ]**)
- "hour": careful **[ˈaʊə]** → rapid **[aʊɹ]**

### Vowel Reduction

**Rule:** In unstressed syllables, vowels often reduce to schwa **[ə]** or near-close vowels **[ɪ]**, **[ʊ]**.

**English vowel reduction patterns:**

- Full vowel → schwa: "photograph" **[ˈfəʊtəɡɹæf]** vs. "photographer" **[fəˈtɒɡɹəfə]**
- Full vowel → **[ɪ]**: "music" **[ˈmjuːzɪk]** (not **[ˈmjuːzik]**)
- Full vowel → **[ʊ]**: "to" (unstressed) **[tʊ]** or **[tə]**

Examples showing reduction:
- "atom" **[ˈætəm]** - second vowel reduces to schwa
- "connect" **[kəˈnɛkt]** - first vowel reduces to schwa
- "about" **[əˈbaʊt]** - first vowel is schwa
- "sofa" **[ˈsəʊfə]** - second vowel is schwa

German reduction:
- "geben" (to give) **[ˈɡeːbən]** - final syllable reduced to schwa
- "haben" (to have) **[ˈhaːbən]** - final syllable reduced to schwa

## Vocabulary

### Vowel Types
- **Monophthong** - Single, pure vowel with stable quality throughout
- **Diphthong** - Gliding vowel moving from one quality to another
- **Triphthong** - Three-part gliding vowel sequence
- **Tense vowel** - Vowel produced with greater muscular tension (e.g., **[i]**, **[u]**)
- **Lax vowel** - Vowel produced with less tension (e.g., **[ɪ]**, **[ʊ]**)
- **Reduction** - Process where unstressed vowels become schwa or near-close vowels

### Vowel Quality Terms
- **Front rounded** - Vowel with front tongue position and lip rounding (**[y]**, **[ø]**, **[œ]**)
- **Near-close** - Vowel slightly lower than close (cardinal) position
- **Near-open** - Vowel slightly higher than open position
- **Central** - Vowel with tongue between front and back positions

### Phonetic Symbols (Additional Vowels)
- **[ɪ]** - Near-close front unrounded (English "sit")
- **[ʊ]** - Near-close back rounded (English "book")
- **[æ]** - Near-open front unrounded (English "cat")
- **[y]** - Close front rounded (French "tu")
- **[ø]** - Close-mid front rounded (French "peu")
- **[œ]** - Open-mid front rounded (French "peur")
- **[ʏ]** - Near-close front rounded (German "Glück")
- **[ɐ]** - Near-open central (German unstressed syllables)

## Conversation Scenarios

### Distinguishing Vowel Quality

Practice identifying and describing vowel qualities including rounding and tenseness. The tutor will present pairs of vowels for comparison.

Key phrases:
- "**[i]** is tense and close, while **[ɪ]** is lax and near-close"
- "**[y]** is the rounded counterpart of **[i]**"
- "This diphthong glides from open to close"
- "The vowel reduces to schwa in unstressed position"

### Analyzing Diphthongs

Practice breaking down diphthongs into their starting and ending vowel qualities.

Key phrases:
- "The diphthong **[aɪ]** starts at open **[a]** and glides to close front **[ɪ]**"
- "English 'boy' contains the diphthong **[ɔɪ]**"
- "This is a centering diphthong ending in schwa"

## Practice Patterns

1. **Vowel discrimination:** Distinguish **[i]** vs. **[ɪ]**, **[u]** vs. **[ʊ]**, **[ɛ]** vs. **[æ]** in transcriptions
2. **Rounded front vowels:** Produce and identify **[y]**, **[ø]**, **[œ]** in French and German words
3. **Diphthong analysis:** Break down diphthongs into starting + ending points
4. **Transcription practice:** Write full transcriptions with correct vowel symbols: "choice" → **[tʃɔɪs]**
5. **Reduction patterns:** Identify which vowels reduce to schwa in multi-syllable words
6. **Tense/lax pairs:** Practice minimal pairs like "beat" **[biːt]** vs. "bit" **[bɪt]**
7. **Cross-linguistic comparison:** Compare English **[ʊ]** to German **[ʏ]** (different features)

## Common Mistakes to Watch

- **[i] vs. [ɪ]** → English "seat" is **[siːt]** not **[sɪːt]**; "sit" is **[sɪt]** not **[sit]**
- **[y] vs. [ju]** → French "tu" is **[ty]** (single rounded vowel), not **[tju]** (consonant + vowel)
- Writing diphthongs with **[j]** or **[w]** → Use vowel symbols: **[aɪ]** not **[aj]**, **[aʊ]** not **[aw]**
- Confusing **[ø]** and **[œ]** → **[ø]** is close-mid, **[œ]** is open-mid (tongue height difference)
- Using full vowels in unstressed syllables → "about" is **[əˈbaʊt]** not **[æˈbaʊt]**
- **[æ]** vs. **[a]** → English "cat" is **[kʰæt]** (near-open), Spanish "casa" is **[ˈkasa]** (open)
- Forgetting vowel length in German → "fühlen" **[ˈfyːlən]** needs **[ː]**, "füllen" **[ˈfʏlən]** is short
- Triphthong over-precision → "fire" is often **[faɪɹ]** in natural speech, not always **[faɪə]**
- Confusing **[ʊ]** and **[ʏ]** → **[ʊ]** is back rounded, **[ʏ]** is front rounded (different backness)
- Not recognizing schwa → "sofa" ends with schwa **[ˈsəʊfə]**, not a full vowel

## Cultural Notes

- Rounded front vowels **[y]**, **[ø]**, **[œ]** are difficult for English speakers but common in French, German, Turkish, and many other languages
- The tense/lax vowel distinction is crucial in English but not significant in Spanish or Italian
- British and American English have different diphthong realizations, especially for vowels in "goat" and "face"
- Vowel reduction to schwa is a hallmark of English rhythm, creating the characteristic stress-timed pattern
- German has systematic short/long vowel pairs, where length often correlates with vowel quality (tense long vs. lax short)
- Some languages (Finnish, Japanese) have pure monophthongs only, with no diphthongs
- The distinction between **[ɑ]** and **[a]** separates British and American English "father" pronunciations
