## Lesson Goals

- Understand the purpose and structure of the International Phonetic Alphabet
- Learn the cardinal vowel chart and its organization principles
- Recognize the difference between phonetics and phonology
- Master basic IPA transcription conventions (brackets, slashes)
- Identify primary cardinal vowels on the vowel trapezium

## Grammar Focus

### What is the IPA?

**Rule:** The International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) is a standardized system of phonetic notation designed to represent all speech sounds of human languages using unique symbols.

Key principles:
- One symbol represents one sound (one-to-one correspondence)
- Symbols are based primarily on the Latin alphabet with Greek and invented characters
- The IPA is language-neutral and universal
- First published in 1888, regularly updated by the International Phonetic Association

**Notation conventions:**

- **[square brackets]** - Phonetic transcription (exact pronunciation): **[kæt]** (cat)
- **/slashes/** - Phonemic transcription (abstract sound units): **/kæt/** (cat)

Examples:
- English "cat": **[kʰæt]** (with aspiration detail) vs. **/kæt/** (phonemic)
- Spanish "casa" (house): **[ˈka.sa]** (phonetic with syllable boundary)
- French "tu" (you): **[ty]** (rounded front vowel)
- German "ich" (I): **[ɪç]** (voiceless palatal fricative)
- Japanese "学生" (student): **[ɡakɯseː]** (with length marker)

### The Cardinal Vowel System

**Rule:** Cardinal vowels are a set of reference vowels defined by precise tongue and lip positions, not by any specific language. They form the framework for describing all vowel sounds.

The vowel chart (trapezium) has two dimensions:
1. **Vertical axis (height):** tongue height (close/high → open/low)
2. **Horizontal axis (backness):** tongue position (front → central → back)

**Primary Cardinal Vowels (1-8):**

| Number | Symbol | Description | Approximate language example |
|--------|--------|-------------|------------------------------|
| 1 | **[i]** | Close front unrounded | Spanish "sí", French "si" |
| 2 | **[e]** | Close-mid front unrounded | Spanish "peso", French "thé" |
| 3 | **[ɛ]** | Open-mid front unrounded | English "bed", French "bête" |
| 4 | **[a]** | Open front unrounded | Spanish "casa", French "patte" |
| 5 | **[ɑ]** | Open back unrounded | British English "father" |
| 6 | **[ɔ]** | Open-mid back rounded | British English "thought" |
| 7 | **[o]** | Close-mid back rounded | Spanish "poco", French "eau" |
| 8 | **[u]** | Close back rounded | Spanish "tú", French "tout" |

**Vowel height terminology:**

- **Close** (high): **[i]**, **[u]** - tongue near roof of mouth
- **Close-mid**: **[e]**, **[o]** - tongue slightly lower
- **Open-mid**: **[ɛ]**, **[ɔ]** - tongue significantly lower
- **Open** (low): **[a]**, **[ɑ]** - tongue at lowest position

**Backness terminology:**

- **Front**: **[i]**, **[e]**, **[ɛ]**, **[a]** - tongue body forward
- **Back**: **[u]**, **[o]**, **[ɔ]**, **[ɑ]** - tongue body retracted

**Lip rounding:**

- **Unrounded**: **[i]**, **[e]**, **[ɛ]**, **[a]**, **[ɑ]** - lips spread or neutral
- **Rounded**: **[u]**, **[o]**, **[ɔ]** - lips pursed

Examples with multiple features:
- **[i]** = close + front + unrounded (like "ee" in English "see")
- **[u]** = close + back + rounded (like "oo" in English "boot")
- **[ɑ]** = open + back + unrounded (British English "father")
- **[ɔ]** = open-mid + back + rounded (British English "thought")

### Central Vowels

**Rule:** Central vowels have the tongue positioned between front and back. The most important is schwa **[ə]**.

Common central vowels:
- **[ə]** - Mid central (schwa): English "about" **[əˈbaʊt]**, German "bitte" **[ˈbɪtə]**
- **[ɜ]** - Open-mid central unrounded: British English "bird" **[bɜːd]**
- **[ʌ]** - Open-mid back unrounded: English "but" **[bʌt]**
- **[ɨ]** - Close central unrounded: Russian "ы" **[ɨ]**
- **[ʉ]** - Close central rounded: Swedish "hus" (house)

**Schwa** is the most common vowel in human languages, typically occurring in unstressed syllables.

Examples:
- English "banana": **[bəˈnænə]** (two schwas in unstressed positions)
- German "geben" (to give): **[ˈɡeːbən]** (schwa in second syllable)

### Vowel Length and Nasalization

**Rule:** IPA uses diacritics to mark additional vowel features like length and nasalization.

**Length markers:**

- **[ː]** - Long vowel (triangular colon): German "Beet" (flowerbed) **[beːt]** vs. "Bett" (bed) **[bɛt]**
- **[ˑ]** - Half-long (optional): some varieties mark intermediate length

**Nasalization:**

- **[◌̃]** - Nasal vowel (tilde over vowel): French "bon" (good) **[bɔ̃]**, "vin" (wine) **[vɛ̃]**

Examples contrasting oral vs. nasal:
- French "beau" (beautiful) **[bo]** vs. "bon" (good) **[bɔ̃]**
- Portuguese "mão" (hand) **[mɐ̃w̃]** - nasal diphthong

## Vocabulary

### IPA Terminology
- **Phonetics** - The study of speech sounds and their physical production
- **Phonology** - The study of sound patterns and abstract sound systems
- **Transcription** - Writing spoken language using phonetic symbols
- **Cardinal vowel** - Reference point vowel defined by articulatory position
- **Trapezium** - Vowel chart diagram (also called "vowel quadrilateral")
- **Schwa** - Mid-central vowel **[ə]**, most common vowel in languages

### Articulatory Terms
- **Close** - Vowel with tongue near roof of mouth (also "high")
- **Open** - Vowel with tongue low in mouth (also "low")
- **Front** - Vowel with tongue positioned forward
- **Back** - Vowel with tongue positioned toward rear
- **Rounded** - Vowel produced with lip rounding
- **Unrounded** - Vowel produced without lip rounding
- **Central** - Vowel with tongue between front and back positions

### Notation Symbols
- **[brackets]** - Phonetic transcription (precise detail)
- **/slashes/** - Phonemic transcription (abstract categories)
- **[ː]** - Length marker (long vowel)
- **[ˑ]** - Half-length marker
- **[◌̃]** - Nasalization diacritic (tilde)

## Conversation Scenarios

### Identifying Vowel Positions

Practice describing vowel articulations using height, backness, and rounding terminology. The tutor will present IPA symbols and ask you to describe their features.

Key phrases:
- "The symbol **[i]** is a close front unrounded vowel"
- "**[u]** is close, back, and rounded"
- "Schwa **[ə]** is mid central"
- "This vowel is open-mid and front"

### Reading Phonetic Transcriptions

Practice reading simple IPA transcriptions aloud and identifying the language or word.

Key phrases:
- "This transcription represents the word..."
- "The long vowel is marked with **[ː]**"
- "I see a nasalized vowel marked with tilde"
- "The schwa appears in the unstressed syllable"

## Practice Patterns

1. **Vowel chart navigation:** Point to positions on the vowel trapezium and name the corresponding cardinal vowel
2. **Feature identification:** Given a vowel symbol, state its height, backness, and rounding
3. **Symbol recognition:** Match IPA symbols to their cardinal vowel numbers (1-8)
4. **Transcription reading:** Read simple transcriptions aloud: **[papa]**, **[mama]**, **[kasa]**
5. **Length distinction:** Identify length markers in transcriptions: **[bit]** vs. **[biːt]**
6. **Notation practice:** Write phonetic transcriptions using correct **[brackets]** vs. **/slashes/**
7. **Schwa identification:** Find schwa **[ə]** in multi-syllable transcriptions

## Common Mistakes to Watch

- **[i] vs. [ɪ]** → **[i]** is fully close (Spanish "sí"), **[ɪ]** is near-close (English "sit")
- **[e] vs. [ɛ]** → **[e]** is close-mid (Spanish "peso"), **[ɛ]** is open-mid (English "bed")
- **[a] vs. [ɑ]** → **[a]** is front open, **[ɑ]** is back open (different tongue positions)
- Using **/slashes/** for phonetic detail → Use **[brackets]** for phonetic transcription
- Confusing **[o]** and **[ɔ]** → **[o]** is close-mid (Spanish "poco"), **[ɔ]** is open-mid (English "thought")
- Forgetting length marker → German "Beet" **[beːt]** needs **[ː]**, not **[bet]**
- Placing nasalization incorrectly → Tilde goes **over** the vowel: **[ɔ̃]**, not **[ɔn]**
- Confusing **[u]** with **[ʊ]** → **[u]** is fully close (Spanish "tú"), **[ʊ]** is near-close (English "book")
- Writing schwa as "uh" → Always use **[ə]** symbol, not English spelling
- Misunderstanding cardinal vowels → They are **reference points**, not specific to one language

## Cultural Notes

- The IPA was created in 1888 by French and British language teachers to have a universal writing system for pronunciation
- The International Phonetic Association maintains and updates the IPA chart (current version from 2015, revised 2018)
- Different linguistic traditions use IPA differently: American linguists often use additional symbols or modifications
- Many dictionaries use modified or simplified IPA systems rather than full IPA (e.g., Webster's uses different symbols)
- The IPA can transcribe any human language sound, including endangered languages without writing systems
- Phoneticians use the IPA to document dialects and accents with precision
- The cardinal vowel system was developed by Daniel Jones in the early 20th century as acoustic reference points
