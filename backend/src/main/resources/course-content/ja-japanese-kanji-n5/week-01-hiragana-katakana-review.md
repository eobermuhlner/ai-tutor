## This Week's Goals

- Review hiragana and katakana to ensure reading fluency
- Understand the concept and purpose of kanji
- Learn basic kanji stroke order rules
- Start with first 100 basic kanji characters

## Grammar Focus

**Minimal grammar**: Focus on recognizing and reading kanji rather than complex grammatical structures.

**Basic particle introduction**:
- **は (wa)** - topic marker
- **を (wo)** - object marker
- **に (ni)** - direction/time marker

Examples:
- **私は学生です** - Watashi wa gakusei desu (I am a student)
- **本を読みます** - Hon wo yomimasu (I read a book)
- **学校に行きます** - Gakkou ni ikimasu (I go to school)

## Conversation Scenarios

Practice identifying and reading basic kanji in simple contexts. The tutor will help you recognize and pronounce these characters.

Topics to cover:
- Counting numbers with kanji
- Identifying body parts
- Recognizing family relationships
- Describing basic objects

Tutor will prompt: "Can you read this kanji?" or "What does this character mean?"

## Practice Patterns

1. Write each kanji 3 times with correct stroke order
2. Read the kanji aloud with correct pronunciation
3. Create simple sentences using the new kanji
4. Match kanji to their meanings
5. Practice reading short phrases with the new characters
6. Connect kanji to hiragana readings

## Common Mistakes to Watch

- **Wrong stroke order** → Remember: top to bottom, left to right!
- **Confusing similar kanji** → 人 (person) vs 入 (enter)
- **Forgetting on'yomi vs kun'yomi** → 学 (がく/gaku) vs (まなぶ/manabu)
- **Misreading radicals** → Always look for familiar components first
- **Incomplete strokes** → Each stroke should be clear and complete

## Cultural Notes

- Stroke order is crucial in Japanese - it affects both legibility and computer input
- Kanji characters were originally borrowed from China but adapted to Japanese
- Radicals (components of kanji) often give clues to the character's meaning
- There are 2,136 official "Jōyō" kanji that Japanese students learn by the end of high school
- The N5 level covers the 80 most basic kanji characters
- Japanese people often switch between kanji, hiragana, and katakana in daily writing
