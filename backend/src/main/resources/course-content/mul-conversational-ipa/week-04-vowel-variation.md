## Grammar Focus

### Vowel Length Distinctions

**Rule:** Many languages use vowel length (duration) to distinguish words. Marked with **[ː]** (long) after the vowel.

**Languages with length contrasts:**

**Japanese** (crucial distinction):
- "おばさん" (aunt) **[obaːsaɴ]** - one long **[aː]**
- "おばあさん" (grandmother) **[obaːːsaɴ]** - two long vowels **[aːː]** (ultra-long!)
- "おじさん" (uncle) **[odʑisaɴ]** - short **[i]**
- "おじいさん" (grandfather) **[odʑiːsaɴ]** - long **[iː]**

**German** (length + quality):
- "Beet" (flowerbed) **[beːt]** - long close **[eː]**
- "Bett" (bed) **[bɛt]** - short open **[ɛ]**
- "Ofen" (oven) **[ˈoːfn̩]** - long **[oː]**
- "offen" (open) **[ˈɔfn̩]** - short **[ɔ]**
- "Staat" (state) **[ʃtaːt]** - long **[aː]**
- "Stadt" (city) **[ʃtat]** - short **[a]**

**Finnish** (consonant + vowel length):
- "tuli" (fire) **[ˈtuli]** - short vowel
- "tuuli" (wind) **[ˈtuːli]** - long vowel **[uː]**
- "tulli" (customs) **[ˈtulːi]** - long consonant **[lː]**

**Arabic:**
- "kataba" (he wrote) **[ˈkataba]** - short vowels
- "kaataba" (he corresponded) **[ˈkaːtaba]** - long **[aː]**

Reading tip: **[ː]** is a triangular colon, not regular colon **[:]**

### Nasal Vowels

**Rule:** Nasal vowels have air flowing through the nose during vowel production. Marked with tilde **[◌̃]** over the vowel.

**French** (4 nasal vowels):
- **[ɛ̃]** - "vin" (wine) **[vɛ̃]**, "pain" (bread) **[pɛ̃]**, "main" (hand) **[mɛ̃]**
- **[ɑ̃]** - "sans" (without) **[sɑ̃]**, "temps" (time) **[tɑ̃]**, "vent" (wind) **[vɑ̃]**
- **[ɔ̃]** - "bon" (good) **[bɔ̃]**, "nom" (name) **[nɔ̃]**, "pont" (bridge) **[pɔ̃]**
- **[œ̃]** - "un" (one) **[œ̃]**, "brun" (brown) **[bʁœ̃]** - disappearing in modern French

**French phrases:**
- "un bon vin blanc" (a good white wine) **[œ̃ bɔ̃ vɛ̃ blɑ̃]** - all four nasal vowels!
- "enchanté" (nice to meet you) **[ɑ̃ʃɑ̃ˈte]** - two **[ɑ̃]**

**Portuguese** (extensive nasal system):
- "mão" (hand) **[mɐ̃w̃]** - nasal diphthong
- "pão" (bread) **[pɐ̃w̃]**
- "não" (no/not) **[nɐ̃w̃]**
- "mãe" (mother) **[mɐ̃j̃]**
- "põe" (puts) **[põj̃]**

**Polish:**
- "ą" = **[ɔ̃]**: "są" (they are) **[sɔ̃]**
- "ę" = **[ɛ̃]**: "węgiel" (coal) **[ˈvɛ̃ɡʲɛl]**

### Central Vowels and Schwa

**Rule:** Central vowels have tongue positioned between front and back. Schwa **[ə]** is the most common.

**Schwa and variants:**
- **[ə]** - Mid-central schwa: English "about" **[əˈbaʊt]**, "sofa" **[ˈsoʊfə]**
- **[ɐ]** - Near-open central: German "besser" **[ˈbɛsɐ]**, Portuguese "mão" **[mɐ̃w̃]**
- **[ɜ]** - Open-mid central: British English "bird" **[bɜːd]**, "girl" **[ɡɜːl]**
- **[ɨ]** - Close central unrounded: Russian "ы" **[ɨ]**, Romanian "â" **[ɨ]**
- **[ʉ]** - Close central rounded: Swedish "hus" (house) **[hʉːs]**

**English schwa** (most common vowel):
- "banana" **[bəˈnænə]** - two schwas
- "photography" **[fəˈtɑɡɹəfi]** - two schwas
- "the" **[ðə]** (weak form) - schwa

**Rhotic schwas** (American English):
- **[ɚ]** - R-colored schwa: "butter" **[ˈbʌɾɚ]**, "mother" **[ˈmʌðɚ]**
- **[ɝ]** - R-colored mid-central: "bird" **[bɝd]**, "word" **[wɝd]**

**Russian [ɨ]:**
- "ты" (you) **[tɨ]**
- "мы" (we) **[mɨ]**
- "сын" (son) **[sɨn]**

### Diphthongs Across Languages

**Rule:** Diphthongs are vowels that glide from one quality to another within a syllable.

**English diphthongs:**
- **[aɪ]** - "price" **[pɹaɪs]**, "my" **[maɪ]**, "igh" **[haɪ]**
- **[aʊ]** - "mouth" **[maʊθ]**, "now" **[naʊ]**, "house" **[haʊs]**
- **[eɪ]** - "face" **[feɪs]**, "day" **[deɪ]**, "great" **[ɡɹeɪt]**
- **[oʊ]** (US) / **[əʊ]** (UK) - "goat" **[ɡoʊt]** / **[ɡəʊt]**, "no" **[noʊ]** / **[nəʊ]**
- **[ɔɪ]** - "choice" **[tʃɔɪs]**, "boy" **[bɔɪ]**, "noise" **[nɔɪz]**

**German diphthongs:**
- **[aɪ]** - "nein" (no) **[naɪn]**, "mein" (my) **[maɪn]**
- **[aʊ]** - "Haus" (house) **[haʊs]**, "Frau" (woman) **[fɹaʊ]**
- **[ɔʏ]** - "heute" (today) **[ˈhɔʏtə]**, "neu" (new) **[nɔʏ]**

**Spanish** (has fewer diphthongs):
- **[ai]** - "baile" (dance) **[ˈbaile]**
- **[au]** - "auto" (car) **[ˈauto]**
- **[ei]** - "seis" (six) **[seis]**
- **[oi]** - "oigo" (I hear) **[ˈoiɣo]**
- **[ue]** - "bueno" (good) **[ˈbweno]** (rising diphthong)

**French** (few true diphthongs):
- **[wa]** - "moi" (me) **[mwa]**, "toi" (you) **[twa]**
- **[ɥi]** - "nuit" (night) **[nɥi]**, "huit" (eight) **[ɥit]**
- **[wɛ̃]** - "loin" (far) **[lwɛ̃]**

### Vowel Reduction Patterns

**Rule:** Unstressed vowels often reduce to schwa or are deleted entirely.

**English reduction:**
- "photograph" **[ˈfoʊtəɡɹæf]** → "photographer" **[fəˈtɑɡɹəfɚ]** (vowel reduces)
- "can" (strong) **[kæn]** → (weak) **[kən]** or **[kn̩]**
- "and" (strong) **[ænd]** → (weak) **[ənd]** or **[ən]** or **[n̩]**

**Russian vowel reduction:**
- "молоко" (milk) **[məlɐˈko]** - unstressed "o" → **[ə]** or **[ɐ]**
- "хорошо" (good) **[xərɐˈʃo]** - reduction of unstressed vowels

**German reduction:**
- Final "-e" always schwa: "Katze" (cat) **[ˈkatsə]**, "geben" (to give) **[ˈɡeːbən]**

## Conversation Scenarios

### Reading Length Distinctions

Practice reading transcriptions with length markers, paying attention to duration.

Key phrases to practice:
- German "Beet" **[beːt]** vs. "Bett" **[bɛt]**
- Japanese "おばさん" **[obaːsaɴ]** vs. "おばあさん" **[obaːːsaɴ]**
- "The colon **[ː]** means the vowel is held longer"

### Reading Nasal Vowels

Practice reading French and Portuguese nasal vowels, focusing on nasal airflow.

Key phrases to practice:
- French "bon vin" **[bɔ̃ vɛ̃]**
- "The tilde **[◌̃]** means air flows through the nose"
- Portuguese "não" **[nɐ̃w̃]**

### Distinguishing Diphthongs

Practice reading diphthong transcriptions and distinguishing glide direction.

Key phrases to practice:
- English "price" **[pɹaɪs]** - glides from **[a]** to **[ɪ]**
- German "heute" **[ˈhɔʏtə]** - diphthong **[ɔʏ]**
- "This diphthong starts low and ends high"

## Practice Patterns

1. **Length recognition:** Identify **[ː]** markers in transcriptions
2. **Nasal vowel reading:** Pronounce French **[ɛ̃]**, **[ɑ̃]**, **[ɔ̃]**
3. **Schwa identification:** Find **[ə]** in English multi-syllable words
4. **Diphthong direction:** Trace glide paths in **[aɪ]**, **[aʊ]**, **[eɪ]**
5. **Central vowel distinction:** Distinguish **[ə]**, **[ɐ]**, **[ɜ]**, **[ɨ]**
6. **Length contrasts:** Compare German long/short pairs
7. **Reduction patterns:** Identify reduced vs. full vowels in connected speech

## Common Mistakes to Watch

- Forgetting length marker → German "Beet" needs **[beːt]** not **[bet]**
- Confusing **[ː]** and regular colon → Use triangular **[ː]** for length
- Missing nasal tilde → French "bon" is **[bɔ̃]** not **[bon]** (nasal vs. oral)
- **[ɛ̃] vs. [ɑ̃] vs. [ɔ̃]** → Different French nasal vowels (vin/sans/bon)
- Schwa as full vowel → "about" is **[əˈbaʊt]** not **[æˈbaʊt]**
- **[ɚ] vs. [ɝ]** → **[ɚ]** is r-colored schwa, **[ɝ]** is r-colored **[ɜ]**
- **[ɨ] vs. [ɪ]** → **[ɨ]** is central (Russian "ы"), **[ɪ]** is front (English "sit")
- Diphthong as two vowels → **[aɪ]** glides smoothly, not **[a]** + **[i]** separately
- **[ɐ] vs. [ə]** → **[ɐ]** is lower (near-open), **[ə]** is mid
- Not recognizing vowel reduction → "and" weak form is **[ənd]** or **[ən]**, not **[ænd]**

## Cultural Notes

- Japanese vowel length is phonemic: changing length changes meaning entirely
- French nasal vowels developed historically when final nasal consonants were lost
- English has more diphthongs than most languages - pure monophthongs are rare
- German long vowels are often tense/close, short vowels are lax/open (coupled features)
- The schwa **[ə]** is the most common vowel in English but has no dedicated letter
- Portuguese has one of the most complex nasal vowel systems among European languages
- Russian **[ɨ]** has no equivalent in Western European languages - challenging for learners
- Vowel reduction creates the stress-timed rhythm of English (unlike syllable-timed Spanish)
